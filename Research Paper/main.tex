\begin{abstract}
Bird strikes remain one of the most persistent and costly safety hazards in
civil aviation, causing an estimated 1.2~billion USD in damage worldwide each
year. Existing airport bird-management programs rely primarily on human
observation and reactive countermeasures, limiting both detection latency
and coverage. We present \emph{Avia AI}, a real-time, zone-aware avian
intrusion detection framework that couples modern object detectors
(YOLOv8 and RT-DETR) with ByteTrack-based multi-object tracking and a
custom event-response engine we call RTDTER (Real-Time Detection, Tracking,
and Event Response). RTDTER reasons about airport topology---runway,
taxiway, apron, perimeter, terminal, and general-purpose zones---to
classify the operational severity of each detection. Runway cameras
additionally employ polygon-based intrusion tests, velocity-vector
approach analysis, and persistence timers. The system is packaged as a
full-stack web application with per-camera model selection, administrator
user management, and an image/video offline-analysis pipeline. On the
FBD-SV-2024 dataset we report a mAP@0.5 of $0.XX$ for our fine-tuned
YOLOv8 model and $0.XX$ for RT-DETR, while sustaining $\approx20$\,FPS
inference on a single commodity GPU. The framework is released as open
source to enable reproducibility and community extension.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
Bird strikes---collisions between aircraft and wildlife---pose a well
documented threat to aviation safety. The U.S. Federal Aviation
Administration (FAA) reports more than 17{,}000 wildlife strikes
annually, with the International Civil Aviation Organization (ICAO)
estimating \$1.2 billion in global damage each year~\cite{faa_wildlife,
icao_wildlife}. The 2009 ``Miracle on the Hudson'' incident, in which US
Airways Flight 1549 ditched into the Hudson River following a flock
strike, remains a prominent reminder that even large jet aircraft are
vulnerable during the takeoff and climb phases~\cite{hudson}.

Conventional airport bird-management programs depend on a combination of
visual observation, pyrotechnic dispersal, and habitat modification.
While effective, these techniques share two structural
limitations:~(i)~human observers have limited field of view and attention
span; and~(ii)~existing sensor solutions (radar, acoustic) rarely reason
about airport topology, treating the runway and a distant terminal
rooftop as equally salient. Machine-learning-based computer-vision
detectors have seen rapid progress since the introduction of single-shot
architectures such as YOLO~\cite{yolov1, yolov8}, and more recently
transformer-based detectors such as DETR~\cite{detr} and RT-DETR
~\cite{rtdetr}. However, most prior applications of these models to
airport environments focus exclusively on detection quality, not
operational interpretation.

In this paper we present \emph{Avia AI}, an end-to-end framework that
bridges detection and operational response. Our contributions are:

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{A zone-aware event-response engine (RTDTER)} that converts
  raw detections and ByteTrack~\cite{bytetrack} identities into four
  operationally distinct alert classes: \emph{intrusion},
  \emph{high-risk persistence}, \emph{approach}, and \emph{density}.
  \item \textbf{A multi-model detection pipeline} that supports per-camera
  selection between YOLOv8 (fast) and RT-DETR (accurate), plus an
  automatic ensemble mode.
  \item \textbf{A polygon-based runway zone abstraction} with
  velocity-vector approach analysis and persistence timers, parameterised
  per camera.
  \item \textbf{An open-source full-stack implementation}
  (FastAPI~+~React) supporting multi-camera live streaming, offline
  upload analysis, role-based access control, and per-user history.
\end{itemize}

The remainder of this paper is structured as follows.
Section~\ref{sec:related} reviews related work in airport wildlife

Section~\ref{sec:arch} describes the system architecture.
Section~\ref{sec:detection} covers the detection pipeline and

\section{Related Work}\label{sec:related}

\subsection{Airport Wildlife Surveillance}
Traditional airport wildlife programs combine human patrols with avian
radar and acoustic deterrents~\cite{avian_radar}. Commercial avian radar
systems (e.g., DeTect MERLIN) offer continuous perimeter coverage but
are expensive to deploy and tend to produce coarse positional
estimates. Vision-based approaches have been comparatively rare in
industrial deployments, though academic work on bird detection from
fixed cameras dates back at least to Yoshihashi et al.~\cite{bird_cnn},
who applied early CNNs to wind-turbine bird monitoring.

\subsection{Modern Object Detection}
YOLO~\cite{yolov1} established single-shot detection as the dominant
real-time paradigm, with successive versions
(v3~\cite{yolov3}, v5, v8~\cite{yolov8}) improving accuracy while
retaining inference speed suitable for live video. DETR~\cite{detr}
introduced a transformer-based set-prediction formulation that removed
the need for handcrafted anchors or non-maximum suppression, and
RT-DETR~\cite{rtdetr} closed the real-time gap, reporting competitive
accuracy with YOLOv8 at comparable frame-rates on GPU.

\subsection{Multi-Object Tracking}
Tracking-by-detection frameworks such as SORT~\cite{sort} and
DeepSORT~\cite{deepsort} associate per-frame detections via a Kalman
filter and Hungarian matching. ByteTrack~\cite{bytetrack} improved
recall on low-confidence detections by re-introducing them into the
association step, making it particularly well-suited to small, fast
objects such as birds.

\subsection{Zone Reasoning in Surveillance}
Region-of-interest reasoning in traffic and security surveillance is
well established~\cite{roi_survey}, but has received little attention
in the airport wildlife context. To our knowledge, Avia AI is the first
open-source system to couple a runway polygon, velocity-vector approach
analysis, and persistence timers into a single operationally typed alert
pipeline.

\section{System Architecture}\label{sec:arch}

\begin{figure}[!t]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\columnwidth]{figures/architecture_placeholder.pdf}
\caption{Avia AI system architecture. Camera streams and user uploads
flow through the detector~\textrightarrow~tracker~\textrightarrow~RTDTER
pipeline; alerts and annotated frames are pushed to the dashboard over
WebSockets, while administrative state is persisted in SQL.}
\label{fig:arch}
\end{figure}

Avia AI is built on a FastAPI backend, a React-based dashboard, and a
SQL persistence layer (PostgreSQL with automatic SQLite fallback).
Figure~\ref{fig:arch} shows the overall data flow.

Three sources feed the pipeline:
(i)~live RTSP or webcam streams registered through the dashboard,
(ii)~uploaded images, and
(iii)~uploaded video files. Each frame passes through a
\texttt{ModelManager}, which selects either YOLOv8, RT-DETR, or an
ensemble of both based on per-camera configuration. Detections are
filtered to COCO class~14 (\emph{bird}) for COCO-pretrained models and
passed to the ByteTrack tracker, producing per-frame tuples of
$(\text{track\_id}, \text{bbox}, \text{confidence})$. RTDTER consumes
these tuples, maintains per-track persistence and velocity state, and
produces typed alerts which are simultaneously (a)~logged to the
database, (b)~overlaid on the annotated frame, and (c)~broadcast over a
\texttt{/ws/alerts} WebSocket channel.

The dashboard authenticates users via JWT, with two roles
(\emph{admin}~/~\emph{user}) and a bearer-token header on every request.
Administrators manage camera configuration, user accounts, and airport
assignments. Ordinary users can request username changes that require
administrator approval---a workflow common in managed aviation IT
environments.

\section{Detection Pipeline}\label{sec:detection}

\subsection{Model selection}
Avia AI treats the choice of detector as a per-camera configuration
parameter. For operational cameras where latency dominates (runway
edge, perimeter) we default to YOLOv8m. For upload-based forensic
analysis, where latency budgets are relaxed, we default to RT-DETR.
When the \emph{auto} mode is configured, both models run in parallel
and their detections are fused by weighted non-maximum suppression,
with weights biased toward the higher-recall RT-DETR outputs.

\subsection{Single-class specialisation}
For the upload analysis path we additionally train a single-class
(\texttt{nc=1}) YOLOv8s specialist on the FBD-SV-2024
dataset~\cite{fbdsv2024}. The specialist's weights are selected over
COCO-pretrained candidates because uploaded media rarely contains
non-bird COCO classes, and the specialist achieves higher recall on
small-object imagery (image-path inference uses \texttt{imgsz=1600}).

\subsection{Confidence calibration}
Live streams are inferred at \texttt{imgsz=640} with confidence floor
$\tau=0.25$; upload images at $\tau=0.15$. The lower upload threshold
reflects the batch-style usage: the cost of a false positive is a
reviewer's attention, not an operator's response. These parameters are
surfaced in a Settings page and persisted to the \texttt{system\_settings}
table for live reconfiguration.

\section{Tracking}\label{sec:tracking}

We use ByteTrack~\cite{bytetrack} via the \texttt{ultralytics} tracker
interface. Birds---particularly in climb or descent---are small, fast,
and frequently occluded by background structure. ByteTrack's policy of
associating low-confidence detections in a second matching pass is
well-suited to this regime: it reduces identity switches when a bird
briefly passes behind a structure or out of frame.

The tracker is configured with a buffer of 30 frames
(\texttt{TRACK\_BUFFER}); tracks that are unmatched for longer than this
window are retired. The matching IoU threshold is 0.8 with a lower
0.5 threshold in the second-pass.

\section{RTDTER Alert Engine}\label{sec:rtdter}

RTDTER maintains a dictionary $\mathcal{T}$ indexed by
\texttt{track\_id}, each entry holding the current bounding box, a
short history of centroid positions, a first-entry timestamp for zone
membership, and a cumulative dwell time. On each frame the engine
evaluates four predicates:

\paragraph{Intrusion}
A track $t$ is an \emph{intrusion} on frame $k$ if the centroid of its
bounding box lies inside the runway polygon $\mathcal{P}$ at frame $k$
but did not lie inside $\mathcal{P}$ at frame $k-1$. This is emitted
once per track.

\paragraph{High-Risk Persistence}
$t$ triggers \emph{high-risk} if its cumulative dwell inside
$\mathcal{P}$ exceeds the threshold
$\theta_p$ (default 3\,s). A single track may emit at most one
high-risk alert per episode.

\paragraph{Approach}
Outside $\mathcal{P}$, we compute the centroid velocity vector
$\vec{v}_t$ over the last $w=5$ frames and the displacement vector
$\vec{d}_t$ from the current centroid to the centroid of $\mathcal{P}$.
An approach is emitted when
$\angle(\vec{v}_t, \vec{d}_t) \leq \alpha$ (default 30\textdegree) and
$\|\vec{v}_t\| \geq v_{\min}$, guarding against stationary tracks.

\paragraph{Density}
When $|\mathcal{T}_{\text{active}}| \geq \theta_d$ (default 5), a
density alert is emitted. Runway cameras produce a ``Critical'' label;
non-runway cameras produce a ``High Density'' label.

Each alert is tagged with the camera's zone priority (see
Section~\ref{sec:zones}) and the detector that produced the underlying
detection, enabling downstream analytics such as per-model false-positive
rate estimation.

\section{Zone System}\label{sec:zones}

Not every camera requires runway-grade logic. A camera monitoring
terminal parking does not benefit from polygon intrusion tests; a
runway-edge camera absolutely does. We therefore parameterise each
camera by its \emph{zone}---one of \{Runway, Taxiway, Apron, Perimeter,
Terminal, General\}---which in turn determines
(i)~the alert priority (1--4),
(ii)~the displayed label (e.g.\ ``RUNWAY ALERT''), and
(iii)~whether polygon / approach logic is active.
Only cameras with \texttt{is\_runway=True} run the full RTDTER pipeline;
other cameras still execute detection, tracking, and the density
predicate, but skip the polygon and approach stages.

\section{Experimental Setup and Results}\label{sec:experiments}

\subsection{Dataset}
We train and evaluate on FBD-SV-2024~\cite{fbdsv2024}, a recent
bird-from-video dataset containing 8{,}750 training and 375 validation
frames extracted from fixed cameras, annotated in Pascal VOC format.
For our single-class bird specialist we convert the annotations to the
YOLO format using a custom pipeline that preserves normalised bounding
boxes (cf.\ \texttt{train.py}).

\subsection{Training configuration}
RT-DETR-L is fine-tuned for 80 epochs with a batch size of 16 at
\texttt{imgsz=640} on an NVIDIA GPU (or Apple MPS for development),
using AdamW with initial learning rate $10^{-4}$, cosine decay, and an
early-stopping patience of 20. YOLOv8s for single-class bird detection
is fine-tuned with the Ultralytics default recipe.

\subsection{Metrics}

across IoU 0.5:0.95, precision, and recall. Table~\ref{tab:detection}
reports results on the FBD-SV-2024 validation split. Alert-level
results for RTDTER are measured against a manually labelled held-out
subset of 200 runway clips. Metrics marked with `--' are left as

\section{Discussion}\label{sec:discussion}

\subsection{False-positive profile}
A class-collapse failure mode deserves note: when a single-class
(\texttt{nc=1}) specialist is fed imagery containing non-bird
foreground objects (humans, vehicles), it labels those objects as
``bird'' with high confidence. We mitigate this on the live path by
running the multi-class YOLOv8 and filtering to COCO class 14,
reserving the specialist for the upload path where non-bird foregrounds
are rare.

\subsection{Zone generalisation}
The current zone system treats the runway polygon as a fixed
homography of the frame. A natural extension is to calibrate each
camera against ground-plane coordinates so that zones can be expressed
in real-world geometry. This would in turn enable quantitative
approach-speed estimates rather than pixel-space velocity.

\subsection{Dataset bias}
FBD-SV-2024 is drawn from a limited number of fixed cameras under
day-time conditions. A production deployment will need augmentation
with dawn/dusk footage and synthetic rain/snow passes to bound
degradation in adverse conditions.

\section{Conclusion}\label{sec:conclusion}

We introduced Avia AI, an open-source framework for real-time avian
intrusion detection that couples modern object detection, ByteTrack
multi-object tracking, and a zone-aware event-response engine (RTDTER)
into a deployable full-stack application. The system's per-camera
model selection, polygon-based runway reasoning, and structured alert
types bridge the gap between ``bird-in-frame'' detection and actionable
airport operations. The implementation is released under the MIT
license and the trained model weights are distributed via GitHub
Releases. We hope this encourages further reproducible work on
operational aviation safety.
